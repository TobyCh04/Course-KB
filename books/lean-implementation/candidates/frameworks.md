# Candidate Frameworks — How to Implement Lean Manufacturing

## F1: Two Pillars of TPS (Ch 2)
- **Structure**: JIT (Just-In-Time) + Jidoka (Autonomation) as the two foundational pillars
- **JIT**: Produce only what is needed, when needed, in the amount needed. Requires: takt time, continuous flow, pull system
- **Jidoka**: Automation with a human touch; machines detect and stop on defects. Requires: automatic defect detection, line shutdown on abnormality
- **When to use**: Any manufacturing system design or improvement initiative
- **When NOT to use**: Non-manufacturing contexts without adaptation
- **Quote**: "The basis of the Toyota Production system is the absolute elimination of waste." (Ohno)

## F2: Little's Law Application (Ch 3)
- **Structure**: Inventory = Throughput × Lead Time. Diagnose why inventory exists by decomposing into throughput and lead time components.
- **When to use**: When inventory levels are excessive or when diagnosing lead time problems
- **When NOT to use**: When inventory is already at minimum required levels
- **Application**: Kanban sizing, finished goods calculations, WIP reduction

## F3: Lean Simplification Framework (Ch 4)
- **Structure**: Three levels — Philosophy → Goals → Implementation
  - Philosophy: Eliminate waste → Reduce lead time → Improve quality
  - Quality Control foundation: Standards → Inspection → Corrective action
  - Quantity Control: Takt time → Pull system → Kanban
- **When to use**: Teaching Lean concepts, structuring a Lean initiative
- **When NOT to use**: When you need specific tactical tools (use individual tools instead)

## F4: Lead Time Decomposition (Ch 5)
- **Structure**: Lead time = Processing time + Waiting time + Move time + Queue time. Typically 95-99% is non-value-added.
- **Seven techniques to reduce lead time**: (from Ch 5)
  1. Reduce setup/changeover times (SMED)
  2. Reduce lot sizes
  3. Reduce work-in-process
  4. Improve equipment reliability (TPM)
  5. Improve quality yields
  6. Level the production schedule
  7. Improve layout (reduce move times)
- **When to use**: When analyzing any production process for improvement
- **When NOT to use**: When lead time is already optimized

## F5: Four Strategies to Becoming Lean (Ch 7)
- **Structure**:
  1. Synchronize supply to customer, externally (takt, safety stock, buffer stock, model mix leveling)
  2. Synchronize production, internally (line balancing, workload leveling)
  3. Create flow (one-piece flow, cells, reduced batch sizes)
  4. Establish pull-demand systems (kanban, supermarkets)
- **Five diagnostic tools**: Takt calculation, time study, balancing analysis, spaghetti diagram, value stream mapping
- **When to use**: Any Lean implementation project
- **When NOT to use**: When the constraint is outside manufacturing (e.g., sales, engineering)
- **Quote**: "The overall approach to make a value stream lean consists of four key strategies."

## F6: Eight-Step Lean Project Prescription (Ch 8)
- **Structure**:
  - Steps 1-3: Systemwide evaluations (cultural change, management commitment, workforce education)
  - Steps 4-8: Value stream evaluations (current state map → future state design → goal setting → implementation → evaluate and repeat)
- **When to use**: Starting a Lean implementation project
- **When NOT to use**: For incremental improvements to an already-Lean system
- **Key decision**: Implement one value stream at a time vs. "clean sweep" (one-at-a-time is recommended)

## F7: Constraint Management (Ch 12)
- **Structure**: Every process has a constraint. Identify → Exploit → Subordinate → Elevate → Repeat
- **Constraint types**: Process step (longest cycle time), moving constraint (from line balancing), sales constraint, policy constraint, raw materials constraint
- **When to use**: When process performance is limited by a bottleneck
- **When NOT to use**: When the system is already balanced and performing to takt
- **Key insight**: "The constraint is not always a step in your process. It can be any aspect that limits your ability to meet your objective."

## F8: Cellular Manufacturing Design (Ch 13)
- **Structure**: Organize machines by product family (not function) into C or U-shaped cells
  - One-piece or small lot flow
  - Right-sized equipment
  - Cross-trained operators
  - Incoming raw materials and outgoing finished goods easily monitored
- **When to use**: When converting from job-shop or flow-line layout to cellular production
- **When NOT to use**: When product variety is too high for family grouping

## F9: House of Lean (Ch 20)
- **Structure** (visual metaphor):
  - Roof: Objectives (Better quality, Faster lead times, Lower cost, Through total elimination of waste)
  - Pillars: JIT + Jidoka (strategies, tactics, skills)
  - Foundation: People (multiskilled workers, problem solving by all) + Stability (5S, standard work, TPM, process simplification, sustaining gains)
- **When to use**: Teaching Lean to employees, structuring a Lean initiative, communicating the big picture
- **When NOT to use**: When you need specific tactical guidance

## F10: Three Fundamental Issues of Cultural Change (Ch 6)
- **Structure**: Three questions that must be answered "yes" before any Lean initiative:
  1. Do we have the leadership to make this a success?
  2. Do we have the motivation to make this a success?
  3. Do we have the necessary problem solvers in place?
- **When to use**: Before starting any Lean initiative
- **When NOT to use**: When culture is already Lean-ready
- **Key insight**: Culture must change BEFORE tools are applied

## F11: Five Tests of Management Commitment (Ch 19)
- **Structure**: Five yes/no questions:
  1. Are you actively studying and working at making your facility Leaner?
  2. Are you willing to listen to critiques and change?
  3. Do you honestly assess your responsiveness and competitiveness globally?
  4. Are you totally engaged (time, presence, attention, support)?
  5. Are you willing to ask, answer, and act on "How can I make this facility more competitive?"
- **When to use**: Before starting a Lean initiative; as a self-assessment tool
- **When NOT to use**: After implementation has started (too late for go/no-go)

## F12: Make-to-Stock vs Make-to-Order Decision (Ch 3)
- **Structure**: 
  - Make-to-Stock: Produce ahead of demand, hold finished goods inventory, use kanban
  - Make-to-Order: Produce after order received, minimal inventory, longer lead time
  - Decision factors: Demand variability, product variety, lead time requirements
- **When to use**: Designing production systems, setting inventory strategy
- **When NOT to use**: When production system is already designed and running
