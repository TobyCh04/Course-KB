# Candidate Counter-Examples — How to Implement Lean Manufacturing

## CE1: Wanting to LOOK Lean vs BE Lean (Ch 17)
- **Description**: Company wants the appearance of Lean without the substance
- **Root cause**: Internal conflicts, insufficient onsite resources, resistance to change
- **Correct approach**: Address cultural issues first; commit to real change, not cosmetic changes
- **Context**: ABC Widgets Co. — after 4 years of Lean initiative, basic problems still persisted
- **Quote**: "They wanted to look Lean more than they wanted to be Lean."

## CE2: Clean Sweep Implementation (Ch 8)
- **Description**: Trying to implement Lean across all value streams simultaneously
- **Root cause**: Overconfidence, desire for quick results, misunderstanding of complexity
- **Correct approach**: Implement one value stream at a time; prove success before expanding
- **Context**: Large, complex plants with multiple value streams
- **Consequence**: Overwhelmed resources, diluted focus, failure to sustain gains

## CE3: Lean Skills Centrally Located (Ch 17)
- **Description**: Lean expertise is at the home office, not at the plant where the work happens
- **Root cause**: Organizational structure, cost-cutting on plant staffing
- **Correct approach**: Put Lean skills where the problems are — at the plant level
- **Context**: ABC Widgets Maquiladora with skeleton technical personnel
- **Consequence**: Problems can't be solved locally; dependence on remote support; slow response

## CE4: Skipping Precursors (Ch 19)
- **Description**: Starting Lean implementation without addressing the five precursors
- **Root cause**: Impatience, desire to show progress, lack of understanding
- **Correct approach**: Assess and address precursors before starting: management commitment, stable processes, reliable suppliers, trained workforce, proper MRP
- **Consequence**: Lean initiative fails; resources wasted; credibility lost
- **Quote**: "The five precursors to Lean are not properly addressed!" (Reason #10 for failure)

## CE5: Not Understanding Quantity Control (Ch 19)
- **Description**: Facility doesn't understand what Lean really is — specifically the concepts of quantity control
- **Root cause**: Superficial understanding, focus on tools rather than philosophy
- **Correct approach**: Educate workforce on Lean philosophy before applying tools
- **Consequence**: Tools applied incorrectly; gains don't sustain
- **Quote**: "The facility really does not understand what a Lean initiative is."

## CE6: Unreliable Suppliers (Ch 19)
- **Description**: Raw materials suppliers are unreliable, causing production disruptions
- **Root cause**: Supplier management not addressed before Lean implementation
- **Correct approach**: Stabilize supply chain before implementing pull systems
- **Context**: One of the 10 most common reasons Lean initiatives fail

## CE7: Inadequate Process Understanding (Ch 19)
- **Description**: Inadequate understanding of process management — stability, capability, bottlenecks, availability
- **Root cause**: Lack of engineering/analytical skills
- **Correct approach**: Train workforce on process analysis before improvement
- **Consequence**: Improvements built on unstable foundations; problems masked

## CE8: Problems Hidden by Inventory (Ch 3)
- **Description**: Using inventory to hide problems instead of solving them
- **Root cause**: Natural tendency to buffer against uncertainty; fear of stopping production
- **Correct approach**: Reduce inventory to expose problems, then solve them permanently
- **Consequence**: Problems never solved; continuous improvement blocked; inventory costs remain high

## CE9: Sustaining Gains Failure (Ch 10)
- **Description**: Making process improvements but not institutionalizing them — gains lost over time
- **Root cause**: "It is simply a lot sexier to move on to the next problem and solve it."
- **Correct approach**: Standardize gains; audit to ensure permanence; fix it right, fix it once
- **Context**: Most common pattern in manufacturing — solving same problems repeatedly
- **Quote**: "If progress is your desire, then you need to fix it right, and fix it only once."

## CE10: Employee Turnover Destroys Gains (Ch 10)
- **Description**: Key employees leave, taking institutional knowledge with them
- **Root cause**: Knowledge not documented; standard work not maintained
- **Correct approach**: Document all improvements; standard work must survive personnel changes
- **Context**: "The most difficult type of change of all"
- **Consequence**: Gains lost when experienced employees depart

## CE11: Policy Constraints (Ch 12)
- **Description**: Company policies inadvertently limit manufacturing performance
- **Root cause**: Policies designed without understanding manufacturing implications
- **Correct approach**: Review all policies for unintended constraints; challenge assumptions
- **Context**: "The most disturbing constraints are often policy constraints."
- **Consequence**: Performance limited by rules, not by physical or technical limitations
